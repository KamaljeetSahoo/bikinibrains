Every bikinibrains lesson is built on the eleven principles on this page. Each one comes from published learning-science research, cited by author and year so that anyone can go and read the source.

We describe what the research found in plain words. We don't turn it into marketing numbers, and we don't claim more than it shows.

Each principle ends with **In a lesson**: what it means for someone writing or reviewing a sheet. The [lesson contract](Lesson-Contract) turns these lines into rules.

## The principles

### 1. Thinking beats doing, and doing beats watching

Learning activities fall into levels. Watching or reading is *passive*. Dragging a slider or pressing Play is *active*: your hands are busy, but you may not be thinking. Predicting, explaining or working something out is *constructive*: you produce something that wasn't on the screen. Each level tends to lead to more learning than the one below it.

*Source:* Chi and Wylie's ICAP framework (2014). Its highest level, *interactive*, means building ideas together with another person; a lesson you do on your own aims for constructive.

**In a lesson:** every step asks for a prediction, a number, a route or a decision. Pressing Play doesn't count as a question.

### 2. Predict before the reveal

Committing to a prediction before you see the outcome changes what you take from it. If the outcome surprises you, you notice the gap between what you expected and what happened, and that gap is where the learning is.

*Source:* Crouch, Fagen, Callan and Mazur (2004) found that physics students who predicted the result of a classroom demonstration before seeing it understood it better than students who only watched, and that those who only watched did little better than students who never saw the demonstration at all.

**In a lesson:** never animate an outcome until the learner has committed to a prediction.

### 3. What you do with a drawing matters more than how it looks

Studies of algorithm visualisations keep finding the same thing: the picture helps when the learner has to do something with it, and helps much less when they only look.

*Source:* Hundhausen, Douglas and Stasko's review of algorithm visualisation studies (2002) found that how learners engaged with a visualisation mattered more than what it showed. Naps and colleagues (2002) set out a scale of engagement, from not looking at all, through viewing, to responding, changing, constructing and presenting.

**In a lesson:** every layer on the drawing comes with something to do with it, and we spend our effort on the questions before we spend it on polish.

### 4. Try first, then be taught

Having a go at a problem before being shown how to solve it can lead to deeper learning than being shown first, even when the first attempt fails. The condition is that the teaching which follows builds on what the learner tried.

*Source:* Manu Kapur's research on productive failure, and Loibl, Roll and Rummel's review (2017) of when problem-solving before instruction helps.

**In a lesson:** open with a challenge before any terms, keep the learner's attempt on the sheet, and come back to it when teaching.

### 5. Guide the discovery

Leaving newcomers to find things out on their own, with little guidance, works poorly. Discovery works when it is guided: a clear task, the right materials, and feedback.

*Source:* Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) on why minimal guidance fails beginners. Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich and Tenenbaum's meta-analysis (2011) found that unassisted discovery did worse than explicit teaching, while guided discovery did better.

**In a lesson:** each step asks one focused question. The free sandbox comes after the lesson, never instead of it.

### 6. Show a worked example, then fade the help

Beginners learn more from studying a worked example than from solving the same problem cold. As they improve, the help should step back, because guidance that helps a beginner gets in the way of someone who already knows. That is the expertise reversal effect.

*Source:* John Sweller's research on worked examples; Renkl and Atkinson (2003) on fading worked steps; Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller (2003) on the expertise reversal effect.

**In a lesson:** the first time a new move appears, the drawing works it through; the next time, it does part of it; then the learner does it alone. Returning readers can skip the questions altogether.

### 7. Recall it, and recall it again later

Pulling an idea out of memory makes it last longer than reading it again does. Spreading that recall out over days works better than cramming it into one sitting.

*Source:* Roediger and Karpicke (2006) on the testing effect. Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham's review (2013) rated practice testing and spaced practice as the most useful of the ten study techniques they examined.

**In a lesson:** end with a recall question, and put practice on short separate sheets that come back days later.

### 8. Cut everything that doesn't carry the idea

People learn more from a picture and words when anything unnecessary is removed, and when labels sit right next to the thing they label. Interesting but irrelevant extras, known as seductive details, make learning worse. Animation is not better than a good still drawing by default.

*Source:* Richard Mayer's research on multimedia learning, including the coherence and spatial contiguity principles and the work on seductive details. Tversky, Morrison and Bétrancourt (2002) on animation.

**In a lesson:** labels go on the drawing next to what they name, nothing is decorative, and motion is used only where the idea is change or cause and effect.

### 9. Productive struggle feels worse than it is

Learning that makes you work can feel less satisfying than a smooth explanation, even when you are learning more.

*Source:* Deslauriers, McCarty, Miller, Callaghan and Kestin (2019) found that students in active-learning classes learned more than students in polished lectures, yet felt they had learned less.

**In a lesson:** show learners the evidence of their progress, and say plainly, early on, that wrong predictions are part of how this works.

### 10. Feeling fluent is not understanding

When something reads smoothly, we tend to believe we have learned it. People also believe they understand how everyday things work until they are asked to explain them.

*Source:* Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell (2013) on the illusions of self-regulated learning. Rozenblit and Keil (2002) on the illusion of explanatory depth.

**In a lesson:** check understanding by asking the learner to use the idea on a new case. Never ask "Did that make sense?"

### 11. AI must not hand out answers

Unguarded AI help can make practice feel easier while making learning worse.

*Source:* Bastani and colleagues (PNAS, 2025) found that high-school students who practised maths with an unrestricted chatbot did better while they had it and, once it was taken away, worse than students who had never had it. A version designed to give hints rather than answers largely avoided the harm.

**In a lesson:** no AI faces learners today. If it ever does, it must work on the drawing and never hand out answers.

## Claims we never make

These claims are common in education marketing. We don't make them on sheets, on the site, in the wiki or in any announcement.

- **"N times more effective."** How much a method helps depends on the learners, the subject and what it is compared with. A single multiplier presented as a promise misleads.
- **Bloom's "2 sigma" as a promise.** Bloom (1984) described the large benefit of one-to-one tutoring as a problem to work on, not a result anyone could buy. Later research has generally found smaller effects. We don't promise tutor-level results.
- **Learning styles.** The idea that people learn best when teaching matches their "visual" or "auditory" style isn't supported by the evidence (Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork, 2008). We draw because the subjects are spatial, not because some people are "visual learners".
- **The "learning pyramid".** The familiar chart saying we remember 10% of what we read and 90% of what we teach has no traceable research behind its percentages.
- **"Built by experts from [famous university]".** We don't borrow authority. We show our work instead, on the site where readers can see it: the principles we teach by, how each lesson was tested, and who reviewed it.

## How we check a lesson against these

Copy this list into the review of any lesson pull request. The number in brackets is the principle it checks.

- [ ] The lesson opens with a challenge the learner can try before any terms, and later steps come back to that attempt. (4)
- [ ] Every step asks the learner to predict, work out, choose or draw something before anything is revealed. (1, 2)
- [ ] No outcome is animated before the learner has committed to an answer. (2)
- [ ] Every layer on the drawing has something for the learner to do with it. (3)
- [ ] Each question is focused, and the free sandbox appears only after the lesson. (5)
- [ ] A new move is worked through the first time, then the help fades. (6)
- [ ] The recap asks the learner to recall, and a practice sheet exists that comes back days later. (7)
- [ ] Labels sit next to what they label, nothing is decorative, and motion appears only where the idea is change. (8)
- [ ] The learner sees evidence of their progress, and the lesson says early on that wrong guesses are expected. (9)
- [ ] The three before-and-after test questions ask the learner to use the idea on a new case, not to recognise a picture. (10)
- [ ] No AI speaks to the learner, and nothing hands out answers. (11)
- [ ] No claim from "Claims we never make" appears on the sheet or around it.
